Review of Important Definitions

Let \(R\) be a ring.

  • A nonzero element \(a\) is a zero divisor if there is a nonzero \(b\) such that \(ab=0\).
  • A unit is an element with a multiplicative inverse.
  • An integral domain is a commutative ring with identity and no zero divisors.
  • A division ring is a ring with identity in which every nonzero element is a unit.
  • A field is a commutative division ring.

Ring Homomorphisms

A map \(\phi : R \to S\) is a ring homomorphism if, for all \(a,b \in R\),

\[ \phi(a+b)=\phi(a)+\phi(b), \qquad \phi(ab)=\phi(a)\phi(b). \]

If \(\phi\) is both one-to-one and onto, it is an isomorphism of rings.

The Kernel of a Homomorphism

For a ring homomorphism \(\phi : R \to S\), define

\[ \ker \phi = \{r \in R : \phi(r)=0\}. \]

Properties of the Kernel

Kernels are subrings: Ring kernels are group kernel for \((R,+)\). Suppose \(a\) and \(b\) are elements of the kernel, then \(\phi(ab)=\phi(a)\phi(b)=0 \cdot0=0\). So \(ab\) is in the kernel.

Closure under multiplication by elemets in \(R\): If \(a \in \ker \phi\) and \(r \in R\), then

\[ \phi(ar)=\phi(a)\phi(r)=0\phi(r)=0, \qquad \phi(ra)=\phi(r)\phi(a)=\phi(r)0=0. \]

Thus, both \(ar\) and \(ra\) are in the kernel.

Ideals

An ideal \(I\) of a ring \(R\) is a subring such that, whenever \(a \in I\) and \(r \in R\),

\[ ar \in I \qquad\text{and}\qquad ra \in I. \]

These conditions make the ideal a two-sided ideal. In a commutative ring, the two conditions are the same.

Every ring has the trivial ideals \(\{0\}\) and \(R\).

Principal Ideals

In a commutative ring \(R\) with identity, an ideal generated by one element \(a\) is called a principal ideal:

\[ \langle a \rangle = \{ar : r \in R\}. \]

For example, in \(\mathbb{Z}\),

\[ \langle n \rangle = n\mathbb{Z}. \]

Every ideal of \(\mathbb{Z}\) is principal.

Example: Reduction Modulo \(n\)

Define \(\phi : \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}_n\) by

\[ \phi(a)=a \pmod n. \]

Reduction modulo \(n\) preserves addition and multiplication, so \(\phi\) is a ring homomorphism. What is the kernel?

Example: Evaluation Homomorphisms

Let \(\mathbb C[a,b]\) be the ring of continuous real-valued functions on \([a,b]\). Fix \(\alpha \in [a,b]\) and define

\[ \phi_\alpha(f)=f(\alpha). \]

Then \(\phi_\alpha:C[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}\) is a ring homomorphism because

\[ \phi_\alpha(f+g)=\phi_\alpha(f)+\phi_\alpha(g), \qquad \phi_\alpha(fg)=\phi_\alpha(f)\phi_\alpha(g). \]

The map sends each function to its value at \(\alpha\). What is the kernel of this homomprhism?

Example: The Kernel of Evaluation

For the evaluation homomorphism \(\phi_\alpha(f)=f(\alpha)\),

\[ \ker\phi_\alpha=\{f\in C[a,b]:f(\alpha)=0\}. \]

So the kernel consists of all continuous functions whose graph passes through \((\alpha,0)\).

Example: Ideals in \(\mathbb{Z}\)

For any integer \(n\), the set

\[ n\mathbb{Z}=\{nk:k\in\mathbb{Z}\} \]

is an ideal of \(\mathbb{Z}\).

If \(a=nk\in n\mathbb{Z}\) and \(r\in\mathbb{Z}\), then

\[ ar=(nk)r=n(kr)\in n\mathbb{Z}. \]

Example: A Principal Ideal

Consider the set of all polynomials in \(x\) over \(\mathbb R\), \(\mathbb{R}[x]\). Is this a ring?

What is the ideal generated by the element \(x\)?

\[ \langle x\rangle=\{x f(x):f(x)\in\mathbb{R}[x]\}. \]

This is exactly the set of polynomials with constant term \(0\). For example, \(x^3-2x\) is in \(\langle x\rangle\).

Quotient Rings

If \(I\) is an ideal of \(R\), the additive cosets form a ring called the quotient ring or factor ring \(R/I\).

Multiplication is defined by

\[ (r+I)(s+I)=rs+I. \]

The ideal condition ensures this product does not depend on which representatives of the cosets we choose.

The Natural Homomorphism

For an ideal \(I\) of \(R\), define

\[ \phi : R \to R/I, \qquad \phi(r)=r+I. \]

This is a surjective ring homomorphism, called the natural or canonical homomorphism.

Its kernel is exactly \(I\):

\[ \ker \phi = \{r \in R : r+I=I\} = I. \]

The First Isomorphism Theorem

Let \(\psi : R \to S\) be a ring homomorphism. Then \(\ker \psi\) is an ideal of \(R\), and

\[ R/\ker\psi \cong \psi(R). \]

The isomorphism is given by

\[ r+\ker\psi \longmapsto \psi(r). \]

The quotient identifies elements of \(R\) that the homomorphism sends to the same value.

–

First Isomorphism Theorem: Commutative Diagram

Let \(\psi:R\to S\) be a ring homomorphism and let \(K=\ker\psi\). The First Isomorphism Theorem gives the following communtative diagram:

\[ \begin{array}{ccc} R & \xrightarrow{\ \psi\ } & \psi(R)\\ {\scriptstyle\phi}\downarrow & \nearrow{\scriptstyle\eta} &\\ R/K && \end{array} \]

  • Canonical homomorphism: \(\phi(r)=r+K\).
  • Induced isomorphism: \(\eta(r+K)=\psi(r)\).

Example: The First Isomorphism Theorem Modulo \(n\)

For \(\phi : \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}_n\) given by reduction modulo \(n\),

\[ \ker\phi=n\mathbb{Z} \qquad\text{and}\qquad \phi(\mathbb{Z})=\mathbb{Z}_n. \]

Therefore, the First Isomorphism Theorem gives

\[ \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}\cong\mathbb{Z}_n. \]

The quotient ring is the image of the homomorphism, with elements identified according to its kernel.

First Isomorphism Theorem: Example with \(R_0\)

Let \[ R_0=\{a+b\varepsilon:a,b\in\mathbb{Z}_3,\ \varepsilon^2=0\}, \] and define \(\psi:R_0\to\mathbb{Z}_3\) by \(\psi(a+b\varepsilon)=a.\)

This is a surjective ring homomorphism with \[ K=\ker\psi=\{0,\varepsilon,2\varepsilon\}=(\varepsilon). \]

The First Isomorphism Theorem gives \(R_0/K\cong\mathbb{Z}_3.\)

The Second Isomorphism Theorem

Let \(I\) be a subring of a ring \(R\), and let \(J\) be an ideal of \(R\). Then \(I\cap J\) is an ideal of \(I\), and

\[ I/(I\cap J)\cong (I+J)/J. \]

The theorem compares a quotient of \(I\) with the image of \(I\) inside the quotient ring \(R/J\).

Second Isomorphism Theorem: Commutative Diagram

Let \(I\) be a subring of \(R\) and \(J\) an ideal of \(R\). The Second Isomorphism Theorem gives the following commutative diagram:

\[ \begin{array}{ccc} I & \xrightarrow{\ q|_I\ } & (I+J)/J\\ {\scriptstyle\phi}\downarrow & \nearrow{\scriptstyle\eta} &\\ I/(I\cap J) && \end{array} \]

  • Quotient map: \(q|_I(i)=i+J\).
  • Canonical homomorphism: \(\phi(i)=i+(I\cap J)\).
  • Induced isomorphism: \(\eta(i+(I\cap J))=i+J\).

Example: The Second Isomorphism Theorem

Take \(R=\mathbb{Z}[x]\), let \(I=\mathbb{Z}\) be the subring of constant polynomials, and let \(J=(x)\).

Every polynomial is a constant plus a multiple of \(x\), so \(I+J=R\). Also, \(I\cap J=\{0\}\).

Thus the theorem gives

\[ \mathbb{Z}/\{0\}\cong \mathbb{Z}[x]/(x). \]

The isomorphism sends the integer \(n\) to the coset \(n+(x)\). In the quotient, every polynomial is identified with its constant term.

The Third Isomorphism Theorem

Let \(J\subseteq I\) be ideals of a ring \(R\). Then \(I/J\) is an ideal of \(R/J\), and

\[ (R/J)/(I/J)\cong R/I. \]

Taking a quotient in two steps gives the same ring as quotienting by the larger ideal at once.

Third Isomorphism Theorem: Commutative Diagram

Let \(J\subseteq I\) be ideals of \(R\). The Third Isomorphism Theorem gives the following communtative diagram:

\[ \begin{array}{ccc} R & \xrightarrow{\ q_J\ } & R/J\\ {\scriptstyle q_I}\downarrow & & \downarrow{\scriptstyle q_{I/J}}\\ R/I & \xrightarrow{\ \eta\ } & (R/J)/(I/J) \end{array} \]

  • Quotient maps: \(q_J(r)=r+J\) and \(q_I(r)=r+I\).
  • Second quotient map: \(q_{I/J}(r+J)=(r+J)+(I/J)\).
  • Induced isomorphism: \(\eta(r+I)=(r+J)+(I/J)\).

Example: The Third Isomorphism Theorem

In \(R=\mathbb{Z}\), choose \(J=6\mathbb{Z}\) and \(I=2\mathbb{Z}\). Since \(6\mathbb{Z}\subseteq2\mathbb{Z}\),

\[ (\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z})/(2\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}) \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}. \]

Here \(2\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}\) is the ideal \(\{0+6\mathbb{Z},\,2+6\mathbb{Z},\,4+6\mathbb{Z}\}\) in \(\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}\).

The Correspondence Theorem

Let \(I\) be an ideal of \(R\). The ideals of \(R\) that contain \(I\) correspond one-to-one with the ideals of \(R/I\).

An ideal \(K\) of \(R\) with \(I\subseteq K\) corresponds to the ideal \(K/I\) of \(R/I\).

Example: Ideals Corresponding in \(\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}\)

The ideals of \(\mathbb{Z}\) that contain \(6\mathbb{Z}\) are

\[ 6\mathbb{Z},\quad 3\mathbb{Z},\quad 2\mathbb{Z},\quad \mathbb{Z}. \]

They correspond to these ideals of \(\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}\):

\[ \{0\},\quad (3),\quad (2),\quad \mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}, \]

respectively.

For example, \(2\mathbb{Z}\) corresponds to

\[ 2\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}=\{0+6\mathbb{Z},\,2+6\mathbb{Z},\,4+6\mathbb{Z}\}. \]
