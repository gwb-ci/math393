Definition of a Group

A group \((G,\circ)\) is a set \(G\) together with a law of composition

\((a,b)\mapsto a\circ b\) satisfying:

  1. Associativity:
    \((a\circ b)\circ c=a\circ(b\circ c)\) for \(a,b,c\in G\).

  2. Identity:
    There exists \(e\in G\) such that \(e\circ a=a\circ e=a\) for every \(a\in G\).

  3. Inverses:
    For each \(a\in G\), there exists \(a^{-1}\in G\) such that \(a\circ a^{-1}=a^{-1}\circ a=e\).

Proposition 3.2.10 — Uniqueness of Identity

The identity element in a group \(G\) is unique.

Suppose \(e\) and \(e'\) are both identity elements.

Since \(e\) is an identity, \(ee'=e'\).

Since \(e'\) is an identity, \(ee'=e\).

Therefore, \(e=ee'=e'\).

Proposition 3.2.11 — Uniqueness of the Inverse

If \(g\) is any element in a group \(G\), then its inverse \(g^{-1}\) is unique.

Suppose \(g'\) and \(g''\) are both inverses of \(g\).

Then \(gg'=g'g=e\) and \(gg''=g''g=e\).

Therefore, \(g'=g'e=g'(gg'')=(g'g)g''=eg''=g''\).

Proposition 3.2.12 — Inverse of a Product

Let \(G\) be a group. If \(a,b\in G\), then \((ab)^{-1}=b^{-1}a^{-1}\).

Indeed,

\(abb^{-1}a^{-1}=aea^{-1}=aa^{-1}=e\),

and similarly,

\(b^{-1}a^{-1}ab=e\).

Thus \(b^{-1}a^{-1}\) is the inverse of \(ab\).

Proposition 3.2.13 — Inverse of an Inverse

Let \(G\) be a group. For any \(a\in G\), \((a^{-1})^{-1}=a\).

Since

\(a^{-1}(a^{-1})^{-1}=e\),

we obtain

\((a^{-1})^{-1}=e(a^{-1})^{-1}=aa^{-1}(a^{-1})^{-1}=ae=a\).

Proposition 3.2.14

Let \(G\) be a group and let \(a,b\in G\). Then the equations \(ax=b\) and \(xa=b\) have unique solutions in \(G\).

For \(ax=b\),

\(x=a^{-1}b\).

For \(xa=b\),

\(x=ba^{-1}\).

Proposition 3.2.15

If \(G\) is a group and \(a,b,c\in G\), then

\(ba=ca\quad\Longrightarrow\quad b=c\)

and

\(ab=ac\quad\Longrightarrow\quad b=c\).

These are the right and left cancellation laws for groups.

For example,

\(ba=ca\quad\Longrightarrow\quad baa^{-1}=caa^{-1}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad b=c\).

Defining Exponents

For \(g\in G\), define

\(g^0=e\),

\(g^n=\underbrace{g\cdot g\cdots g}_{n\text{ times}}\),

and

\(g^{-n}=\underbrace{g^{-1}\cdot g^{-1}\cdots g^{-1}}_{n\text{ times}}\).

Theorem 3.2.16

For all \(g,h\in G\),

  1. \(g^m g^n=g^{m+n}\) for all \(m,n\in\mathbb Z\);

  2. \((g^m)^n=g^{mn}\) for all \(m,n\in\mathbb Z\);

  3. \((gh)^n=(h^{-1}g^{-1})^{-n}\) for all \(n\in\mathbb Z\).

Furthermore, if \(G\) is abelian,

\((gh)^n=g^nh^n\).

Definition of a Subgroup

Let \(G\) be a group.

A subgroup \(H\) of \(G\) is a subset \(H\) of \(G\) such that when the group operation of \(G\) is restricted to \(H\), \(H\) is a group in its own right.

We write

\(H \leq G.\)

A subgroup always uses the same operation as the original group.

Trivial Subgroups

Every group \(G\) has at least two subgroups:

\(\{e\} \leq G\) and \(G \leq G.\)

The subgroup \(\{e\}\) is called the trivial subgroup.

A subgroup \(H\) that is a proper subset of \(G\) is called a proper subgroup.

Subgroups of \(\mathbb U(9)\) Continued

Consider \(\{[1],[5]\}\). \([5] \cdot [5]\) = [7], which is not in the set, so it is not a subgroup.

If we add [7] to the set, we get \(\{[1],[5],[7]\}\). \([5] \cdot [7]\) = [8], which is not in the set, so it is not a subgroup.

If we add [8] to the set, we get \(\{[1],[5],[7],[8]\}\). \([5] \cdot [7]\) = [8], which is in the set, but \([5] \cdot [8]\) = [4], which is not in the set, so it is not a subgroup.

If we add [4] to the set, we get \(\{[1],[4],[5],[7],[8]\}\). \([5] \cdot [7]\) = [8], which is in the set, but \([4] \cdot [5]\) = [2], which is not in the set, so it is not a subgroup.

If we add [2] to the set, we get \(\{[1],[2],[4],[5],[7],[8]\}\), which is \(\mathbb U(9)\), so it is a subgroup.

Subgroups of \(\mathbb Z\)

Consider \(\mathbb Z\) under addition.

For \(n=0,1,2,\ldots\), define

\(n\mathbb Z=\{nk:k\in\mathbb Z\}\).

Examples:

\(2\mathbb Z=\{\ldots,-4,-2,0,2,4,\ldots\}\)

\(3\mathbb Z=\{\ldots,-6,-3,0,3,6,\ldots\}\).

For every \(n\geq 0\),

\(n\mathbb Z\leq\mathbb Z\).

\(12 \mathbb Z \leq 6\mathbb Z \leq 2\mathbb Z \leq \mathbb Z\).

\(\mathbb R^\ast\) and \(\mathbb Q^\ast\)

Consider the group of nonzero real numbers under multiplication:

\(\mathbb R^\ast=\mathbb R\setminus\{0\}\).

Let \(\mathbb Q^\ast=\left\{\frac{p}{q}:p\text{ and }q\text{ are nonzero integers}\right\}\).

Then \(\mathbb Q^\ast\leq\mathbb R^\ast\).

Subgroups of Matrix Groups

Let \(GL_2(\mathbb R)\) be the group of invertible

\(2\times2\) real matrices under matrix multiplication.

Define

\(SL_2(\mathbb R)=\{A\in GL_2(\mathbb R):\det A=1\}\).

Then

\(SL_2(\mathbb R)\leq GL_2(\mathbb R)\).

If

\(A=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}\)

and \(\det A=ad-bc=1\), then

\(A^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}d&-b\\-c&a\end{pmatrix}\).

Also,

\(\det(AB)=\det(A)\det(B)=1\).

Proposition 3.3.7 Subgroup Criterion

A subset \(H\) of \(G\) is a subgroup if and only if it satisfies the following conditions.

  1. The identity \(e\) of \(G\) is in \(H\).

  1. If \(h_1,h_2\in H\), then

    \(h_1h_2\in H\).

  1. If \(h\in H\), then

    \(h^{-1}\in H\).

Associativity does not need to be checked again: it is inherited from \(G\).

Proposition 3.3.7 Subgroup Criterion — Why It Works

Suppose the three conditions hold.

  • \(e\in H\) gives an identity.

  • Closure gives a binary operation on \(H\).

  • \(h\in H\Rightarrow h^{-1}\in H\) gives inverses.

  • Associativity is inherited from \(G\).

Therefore \(H\) is a group under the same operation as \(G\).

\(H\leq G\).

To prove that a subset is a subgroup, we usually do not need to verify all of the group axioms from scratch.

Proposition 3.3.8 Another Subgroup Test

Let \(H\) be a subset of a group \(G\).

Then \(H\) is a subgroup of \(G\) if and only if

\(H\neq\emptyset\)

and whenever \(g,h\in H\),

\(gh^{-1}\in H\).

One condition gives us everything:

\(gg^{-1}=e\in H\),

\(eg^{-1}=g^{-1}\in H\),

and

\(h_1(h_2^{-1})^{-1}=h_1h_2\in H\).

Using the Subgroup Test

Example

Let

\(H=2\mathbb Z\).

Since \(0\in H\), we know \(H\neq\emptyset\).

If \(a,b\in H\), then

\(a=2m,\qquad b=2n\)

for some \(m,n\in\mathbb Z\).

Therefore \(a-b=2m-2n=2(m-n)\in2\mathbb Z\).

Hence \(2\mathbb Z\leq\mathbb Z\).
