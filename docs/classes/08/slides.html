Definition of a Group

A group \((G,\cdot)\) is a set \(G\) together with a law of composition

\((a,b)\mapsto a\cdot b\) satisfying:

  1. Associativity:
    \((a\cdot b)\cdot c=a\cdot(b\cdot c)\) for \(a,b,c\in G\).

  2. Identity:
    There exists \(e\in G\) such that \(e\cdot a=a\cdot e=a\) for every \(a\in G\).

  3. Inverses:
    For each \(a\in G\), there exists \(a^{-1}\in G\) such that \(a\cdot a^{-1}=a^{-1}\cdot a=e\).

Definition of a Subgroup

Let \(G\) be a group.

A subgroup \(H\) of \(G\) is a subset \(H\) of \(G\) such that when the group operation of \(G\) is restricted to \(H\), \(H\) is a group in its own right.

We write \(H \leq G\).

A subgroup uses the same operation as the original group.

Complex Numbers and Operations

The complex numbers are defined as

\[ \mathbb{C}=\{a+bi:a,b\in\mathbb{R}\}, \]

where \(i^2=-1\). If \(z=a+bi\), then \(a\) is the real part of \(z\) and \(b\) is the imaginary part of \(z\).

If \(z=a+bi\) and \(w=c+di\), then

\[ z+w=(a+c)+(b+d)i. \]

Multiplication is given by

\[ zw=(a+bi)(c+di)=(ac-bd)+(ad+bc)i. \]

Multiplicative Inverses and Conjugates

Every nonzero complex number \(z=a+bi\) has a multiplicative inverse:

\[ z^{-1}=\frac{a-bi}{a^2+b^2}. \]

The complex conjugate of \(z=a+bi\) is

\[ \overline{z}=a-bi. \]

The absolute value or modulus of \(z=a+bi\) is \(|z|=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}\).

Examples

For example, if \(z=2+3i\), then

\[ z^{-1}=\frac{2}{13}-\frac{3}{13}i, \qquad |z|=\sqrt{13}, \qquad \overline{z}=2-3i. \]

Polar representation of Complex Numbers

Nonzero complex numbers can also be represented using polar coordinates.

\(z=a+bi=r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)\) where \(a=r\cos\theta\) and \(b=r\sin\theta\), and \(r=|z|=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}\).

Book abbreviates \(r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)\) as \(r\operatorname{cis}\theta\).

We require \(0\leq\theta<2\pi\).

Angle Addition Formulas

For \(\theta, \phi \in \mathbb{R}\),

\[ \sin(\theta+\phi) =\sin\theta\cos\phi+\cos\theta\sin\phi \]

and

\[ \cos(\theta+\phi) =\cos\theta\cos\phi-\sin\theta\sin\phi. \]

Proposition 4.2.5

Let \(z=r\operatorname{cis}\theta\) and \(w=s\operatorname{cis}\phi\) be two nonzero complex numbers. Then

\[ zw=rs\operatorname{cis}(\theta+\phi). \]

Example

If

\[ z=3\operatorname{cis}\left(\frac{\pi}{3}\right) \qquad\text{and}\qquad w=2\operatorname{cis}\left(\frac{\pi}{6}\right), \]

then

\[ zw =6\operatorname{cis}\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right) =6i. \]

DeMoivre’s Theorem (4.2.7)

Let \(z=r\operatorname{cis}\theta\) be a nonzero complex number. Then

\[ [r\operatorname{cis}\theta]^n =r^n\operatorname{cis}(n\theta) \]

for \(n=1,2,\ldots\).

Example

If \(z=1+i\), then

\[ z=\sqrt{2}\operatorname{cis}\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\right). \]

Thus,

\[ z^{10} =(\sqrt{2})^{10}\operatorname{cis}\left(\frac{5\pi}{2}\right) =32\operatorname{cis}\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right) =32i. \]

Proposition 4.2.9

The circle group is

\(\mathbb{T}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|=1\}.\)

is a subgroup of \(\mathbb{C}^*\).

Subgroups of \(\mathbb{T}\)

Consider the complex numbers satisfying \(z^4=1\).

\(H=\{1,-1,i,-i\}\) is a finite subgroup of the circle group.

\(n^{\text{th}}\) Roots of Unity

The complex numbers satisfying the equation

\[ z^n=1 \]

are called the \(n\)th roots of unity.

They lie on the unit circle and are equally spaced around the circle.

A generator for the group of the \(n\)th roots of unity is called a primitive \(n\)th root of unity.

Theorem 4.2.10

If \(z^n=1\), then the \(n\)th roots of unity are

\[ z=\operatorname{cis}\left(\frac{2k\pi}{n}\right), \]

where \(k=0,1,\ldots,n-1\).

Furthermore, the \(n\)th roots of unity form a cyclic subgroup of \(\mathbb{T}\) of order \(n\).

Proof

By DeMoivre’s Theorem,

\[ z^n =\operatorname{cis}\left(n\frac{2k\pi}{n}\right) =\operatorname{cis}(2k\pi) =1. \]

Example 9th roots of unity

The \(9\)th roots of unity satisfy

\[ z^9=1. \]

By Theorem 4.2.10,

\[ z_k=\operatorname{cis}\left(\frac{2k\pi}{9}\right), \qquad k=0,1,\ldots,8. \]

Thus the nine roots are

\[ 1,\; \operatorname{cis}\left(\frac{2\pi}{9}\right),\; \operatorname{cis}\left(\frac{4\pi}{9}\right),\; \operatorname{cis}\left(\frac{6\pi}{9}\right),\; \ldots,\; \operatorname{cis}\left(\frac{16\pi}{9}\right). \]

They form a cyclic subgroup of \(\mathbb{T}\) of order \(9\).

For example,

\[ \omega=\operatorname{cis}\left(\frac{2\pi}{9}\right) \]

is a primitive \(9\)th root of unity, and

\[ \langle\omega\rangle =\{1,\omega,\omega^2,\ldots,\omega^8\}. \]
