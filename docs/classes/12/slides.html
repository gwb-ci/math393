Definition of a Group

A group \((G,\cdot)\) is a set \(G\) together with a law of composition

\((a,b)\mapsto a\cdot b\) satisfying:

  1. Associativity:
    \((a\cdot b)\cdot c=a\cdot(b\cdot c)\) for \(a,b,c\in G\).

  2. Identity:
    There exists \(e\in G\) such that \(e\cdot a=a\cdot e=a\) for every \(a\in G\).

  3. Inverses:
    For each \(a\in G\), there exists \(a^{-1}\in G\) such that \(a\cdot a^{-1}=a^{-1}\cdot a=e\).

Definition of a Subgroup

Let \(G\) be a group.

A subgroup \(H\) of \(G\) is a subset \(H\) of \(G\) such that when the group operation of \(G\) is restricted to \(H\), \(H\) is a group in its own right.

We write \(H \leq G\).

A subgroup uses the same operation as the original group.

Definition of Cosets

Let \(G\) be a group and \(H\) a subgroup of \(G\).

A left coset of \(H\) with representative \(g \in G\) is the set

\(gH = \{gh : h \in H\}\).

A right coset is defined similarly by

\(Hg = \{hg : h \in H\}\).

If left and right cosets coincide, or if it is clear from context which type of coset is meant, we simply use the word coset.

Lemma 6.1.3.

Let \(H\) be a subgroup of a group \(G\) and suppose that \(g_1,g_2\in G\).

The following conditions are equivalent.

  1. \(g_1H=g_2H\);

  2. \(Hg_1^{-1}=Hg_2^{-1}\);

  3. \(g_1H\subset g_2H\);

  4. \(g_2\in g_1H\);

  5. \(g_1^{-1}g_2\in H\).

Theorem 6.1.4

Let \(H\) be a subgroup of a group \(G\).

Then the left cosets of \(H\) in \(G\) partition \(G\).

That is, the group \(G\) is the disjoint union of the left cosets of \(H\) in \(G\).

Proof of 6.1.4

Different proof from the book. Define a relation on \(G\) by \(g_1 \sim g_2\) if and only if \(g_1^{-1}g_2 \in H\). I will show this is an equivalence relation.

Remark 6.1.5

There is nothing special about left cosets. Right cosets also partition \(G\). The proof is the same as for left cosets, except that all group multiplications are done on the opposite side of \(H\).

Theorem 6.1.8

Let \(H\) be a subgroup of a group \(G\).

The number of left cosets of \(H\) in \(G\) is the same as the number of

right cosets of \(H\) in \(G\).

Proof Idea

Let \(L_H\) and \(R_H\) denote the sets of left and right

cosets of \(H\), respectively.

Define \(\phi\:L_H\to R_H\) by \(\phi(gH)=Hg^{-1}\).

Lemma 6.1.3 shows that \(\phi\) is well-defined and one-to-one.

It is onto since \(\phi(g^{-1}H)=Hg\).

Therefore \(\phi\) is a bijection, so the number of left and right cosets

is the same.

Proposition 6.2.1

Let \(H\) be a subgroup of \(G\) with \(g\in G\) and define a map \(\phi:H\rightarrow gH\) by \(\phi(h)=gh\).

The map \(\phi\) is bijective; hence, the number of elements in \(H\) is the same as the number of elements in \(gH\).

Proposition 6.2.1 Proof

We first show that the map \(\phi\) is one-to-one.

Suppose that \(\phi(h_1)=\phi(h_2)\) for elements \(h_1,h_2\in H\).

We must show that \(h_1=h_2\), but \(\phi(h_1)=gh_1\) and \(\phi(h_2)=gh_2\).

So \(gh_1=gh_2\), and by left cancellation \(h_1=h_2\).

To show that \(\phi\) is onto is easy.

By definition every element of \(gH\) is of the form \(gh\) for some \(h\in H\) and \(\phi(h)=gh\).

Lagrange’s Theorem

Let \(G\) be a finite group and let \(H\) be a subgroup of \(G\).

Then \(|G|/|H|=[G:H]\) is the number of distinct left cosets of \(H\) in \(G\).

In particular, the number of elements in \(H\) must divide the number of elements in \(G\).

Lagrange’s Theorem Proof

The group \(G\) is partitioned into \([G:H]\) distinct left cosets.

Each left coset has \(|H|\) elements; therefore,

\[ |G|=[G:H]|H|. \]

Corollary 6.2.3

Suppose that \(G\) is a finite group and \(g\in G\).

Then the order of \(g\) must divide the number of elements in \(G\).

Proof. Apply Lagrange’s Theorem to the subgroup \(\langle g\rangle\).

The order of \(g\) is \(|\langle g\rangle|\), so \(|\langle g\rangle|\) divides \(|G|\).

Corollary 6.2.4

Let \(|G|=p\) with \(p\) a prime number.

Then \(G\) is cyclic and any \(g\in G\) such that \(g\neq e\) is a generator.

Proof. Let \(g\) be in \(G\) such that \(g\neq e\).

Then by Corollary 6.2.3, the order of \(g\) must divide the order of the group.

Since \(|\langle g\rangle|>1\), it must be \(p\).

Hence, \(g\) generates \(G\).

Corollary 6.2.5

Let \(H\) and \(K\) be subgroups of a finite group \(G\) such that \(G\supset H\supset K\).

Then \([G:K]=[G:H][H:K]\).

Proof. Observe that

\[ [G:K] =\frac{|G|}{|K|} =\frac{|G|}{|H|}\cdot\frac{|H|}{|K|} =[G:H][H:K]. \]

Proposition 6.2.7

The group \(A_4\) has no subgroup of order \(6\).

The converse of Lagrange’s Theorem is false.

Although \(6\) divides \(|A_4|=12\), there is no subgroup of \(A_4\) of order \(6\).

Conjugation

For \(g,h\in G\), the element \(ghg^{-1}\) is the conjugate of \(h\) by \(g\).

For a subgroup \(H\) of \(G\), write \(gHg^{-1}=\{ghg^{-1}:h\in H\}\).

Conjugating a cycle applies the conjugating permutation to each entry:

\[ \sigma(a_1\,a_2\,\ldots\,a_k)\sigma^{-1} =(\sigma(a_1)\,\sigma(a_2)\,\ldots\,\sigma(a_k)). \]

For example, \((124)(123)(124)^{-1}=(243)\).

Two cycles \(\tau\) and \(\mu\) in \(S_n\) have the same length if and only if there exists a \(\sigma\in S_n\) such that \(\mu=\sigma\tau\sigma^{-1}\).
