Slides: Fermat and Euler’s Theorems

Class Session Goals

  • Explain why a subgroup of index \(2\) is stable under conjugation.
  • Use Lagrange’s Theorem to derive Euler’s Theorem and Fermat’s Little Theorem.
  • Find conjugacy classes in \(S_4\) and \(A_4\).

Definition of a Group

A group \((G,\cdot)\) is a set \(G\) together with a law of composition

\((a,b)\mapsto a\cdot b\) satisfying:

  1. Associativity:
    \((a\cdot b)\cdot c=a\cdot(b\cdot c)\) for \(a,b,c\in G\).

  2. Identity:
    There exists \(e\in G\) such that \(e\cdot a=a\cdot e=a\) for every \(a\in G\).

  3. Inverses:
    For each \(a\in G\), there exists \(a^{-1}\in G\) such that \(a\cdot a^{-1}=a^{-1}\cdot a=e\).

Definition of a Subgroup

Let \(G\) be a group.

A subgroup \(H\) of \(G\) is a subset \(H\) of \(G\) such that when the group operation of \(G\) is restricted to \(H\), \(H\) is a group in its own right.

We write \(H \leq G\).

A subgroup uses the same operation as the original group.

Definition of Cosets

Let \(G\) be a group and \(H\) a subgroup of \(G\).

A left coset of \(H\) with representative \(g \in G\) is the set

\(gH = \{gh : h \in H\}\).

A right coset is defined similarly by

\(Hg = \{hg : h \in H\}\).

If left and right cosets coincide, or if it is clear from context which type of coset is meant, we simply use the word coset.

Subgroups of Index 2

Let \(H\) be a subgroup of \(G\) with \([G:H]=2\). There are only two left cosets and two right cosets: \(H\) and its complement in \(G\).

Thus \(gH=Hg\) for every \(g\in G\), or equivalently,

\[ gHg^{-1}=H. \]

Proposition 6.2.7

The group \(A_4\) has no subgroup of order \(6\).

Suppose \(|H|=6\). Then \([A_4:H]=2\), so \(gHg^{-1}=H\) for every \(g\in A_4\).

Of the eight \(3\)-cycles in \(A_4\), at least one lies in \(H\). We may take it to be \((123)\). Its inverse \((132)\) also lies in \(H\).

Conjugating by elements of \(A_4\) gives \((243)\) and \((142)\). Their inverses must also lie in \(H\). Together with the identity, these are seven distinct elements, contradicting \(|H|=6\).

The converse of Lagrange’s Theorem is false: \(6\) divides \(|A_4|=12\), but \(A_4\) has no subgroup of order \(6\).

Corollary

If \(G\) is a finite group and \(g\in G\), then

\[ g^{|G|}=e. \]

The order of \(g\) divides \(|G|\), so \(|G|=k|g|\) for some integer \(k\). Hence \(g^{|G|}=(g^{|g|})^k=e\).

The Group \(U(n)\)

\(U(n)\) is the group of units in \(\mathbb{Z}_n\) under multiplication modulo \(n\):

\[ U(n)=\{[a]\in\mathbb{Z}_n:\gcd(a,n)=1\}. \]

For example, \(U(12)=\{[1],[5],[7],[11]\}\).

Each element has a multiplicative inverse modulo \(n\). For instance, \([5][5]=[1]\) in \(\mathbb{Z}_{12}\).

Euler’s Phi Function

The Euler \(\phi\)-function is the map \(\phi:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}\) defined by \(\phi(1)=1\) and, for \(n>1\),

\[ \phi(n)=|\{m:1\leq m<n,\ \gcd(m,n)=1\}|. \]

For example, \(\phi(12)=4\) because \(1,5,7,11\) are relatively prime to \(12\).

If \(p\) is prime, then \(\phi(p)=p-1\).

Theorem 6.3.1

Let \(U(n)\) be the group of units in \(\mathbb{Z}_n\). Then

\[ |U(n)|=\phi(n). \]

The units are precisely the residue classes represented by integers relatively prime to \(n\).

Euler’s Theorem

Let \(a\) and \(n\) be integers such that \(n>0\) and \(\gcd(a,n)=1\). Then

\[ a^{\phi(n)}\equiv 1\pmod n. \]

The residue class of \(a\) belongs to \(U(n)\), which has order \(\phi(n)\). Applying the preceding corollary in \(U(n)\) proves the congruence.

Fermat’s Little Theorem

Let \(p\) be prime. If \(p\nmid a\), then

\[ a^{p-1}\equiv 1\pmod p. \]

Furthermore, for any integer \(b\),

\[ b^p\equiv b\pmod p. \]

Conjugation

For \(g,h\in G\), the element \(ghg^{-1}\) is the conjugate of \(h\) by \(g\).

For a subgroup \(H\) of \(G\), write \(gHg^{-1}=\{ghg^{-1}:h\in H\}\).

Conjugating a cycle applies the conjugating permutation to each entry:

\[ \sigma(a_1\,a_2\,\ldots\,a_k)\sigma^{-1} =(\sigma(a_1)\,\sigma(a_2)\,\ldots\,\sigma(a_k)). \]

For example, \((124)(123)(124)^{-1}=(243)\).

Theorem 6.2.8

Two cycles \(\tau\) and \(\mu\) in \(S_n\) have the same length if and only if there exists a \(\sigma\in S_n\) such that

\[ \mu=\sigma\tau\sigma^{-1}. \]

If \(\tau=(a_1\,\ldots\,a_k)\) and \(\mu=(b_1\,\ldots\,b_k)\), choose \(\sigma\) with \(\sigma(a_i)=b_i\) for each \(i\), then extend it to a permutation of the remaining symbols.

Conversely, conjugating \(\tau\) replaces its entries by their images under \(\sigma\), preserving the cycle length.

Conjugation Is an Equivalence Relation

Define \(h\sim k\) when \(k=ghg^{-1}\) for some \(g\in G\).

  • Reflexive: \(h=ehe^{-1}\).
  • Symmetric: If \(k=ghg^{-1}\), then \(h=g^{-1}kg\).
  • Transitive: If \(k=ghg^{-1}\) and \(\ell=aka^{-1}\), then \(\ell=(ag)h(ag)^{-1}\).

The equivalence classes are called conjugacy classes.

List the elements of \(A_4\) by cycle type. Which \(S_4\) conjugacy classes split when conjugation is restricted to elements of \(A_4\)?

The identity forms one class. The three double transpositions form another. The eight \(3\)-cycles split into two classes of four:

\[ \begin{aligned} [(123)]_{A_4}&=\{(123),(142),(134),(243)\},\\ [(132)]_{A_4}&=\{(132),(124),(143),(234)\}. \end{aligned} \]

Conjugacy Classes and the Center of a Group

The center of a group \(G\) is the set \(Z(G)=\{g\in G:gh=hg\text{ for all }h\in G\}\).

What is can you say about the conjugacy classes of elements in \(Z(G)\)?

Class Equation and Conjugacy Classes in \(S_n\)

Conjugacy classes partition a finite group \(G\). If \(x_1,\ldots,x_k\) represent its classes outside the center, the class equation is

\[ |G|=|Z(G)|+|[x_1]|+\cdots+|[x_k]|. \]

In \(S_n\), permutations are conjugate exactly when they have the same cycle type. If a permutation has \(m_j\) cycles of length \(j\), including fixed points, then

\[ |[\sigma]|=\frac{n!}{\prod_{j=1}^{n}j^{m_j}m_j!}. \]

For example, \((12)(34)\in S_4\) has \(m_2=2\), so its class contains \(\dfrac{4!}{2^2\,2!}=3\) elements.

Activity: Class Equation for \(D_5\)

Class Sizes and Centralizers

For \(x\in G\), its centralizer is \(C_G(x)=\{g\in G:gx=xg\}\).

The conjugacy class of \(x\) has size

\[ |[x]|=[G:C_G(x)]=\frac{|G|}{|C_G(x)|}. \]

In \(S_4\), the permutations commuting with \((123)\) are \((1)\), \((123)\), and \((132)\). Thus

\[ |[(123)]|=\frac{24}{3}=8. \]

The Center of a Finite \(p\)-Group

Theorem. If \(|G|=p^n\) for a prime \(p\), then \(Z(G)\) contains an element other than the identity.

Each conjugacy class outside \(Z(G)\) has size greater than \(1\) and divides \(p^n\). Its size is therefore divisible by \(p\).

The class equation gives

\[ p^n=|Z(G)|+\sum_i |[x_i]|. \]

It follows that \(p\) divides \(|Z(G)|\). Since \(e\in Z(G)\), we have \(|Z(G)|\geq p\).

Groups of Order \(p^2\)

Corollary. Every group of order \(p^2\) is abelian.

The preceding theorem and Lagrange’s Theorem give \(|Z(G)|=p\) or \(p^2\).

Suppose \(|Z(G)|=p\) and choose \(x\notin Z(G)\). The centralizer \(C_G(x)\) contains both \(Z(G)\) and \(x\), so it has more than \(p\) elements. Its order divides \(p^2\), forcing \(C_G(x)=G\).

But then \(x\in Z(G)\), a contradiction. Hence \(Z(G)=G\), so \(G\) is abelian.
