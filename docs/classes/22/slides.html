Class Session Goals

By the end of this session, you should be able to:

  • Define the natural homomorphism, identify its kernel and image, and interpret the commutative diagram in the First Isomorphism Theorem.
  • Apply the First, Second, and Third Isomorphism Theorems and the Correspondence Theorem to relate groups, quotient groups, and subgroups.

Moving from Understanding Groups to Identifying Groups

We now have a good understanding of what groups are and how they work.

We next develop tools to:

  • Identify groups by relating them to familiar groups.
  • Find normal subgroups and understand the quotient groups they produce.

Homomorphisms and the isomorphism theorems that we learn about are starting tools to identify groups.

Recall Theorem 10.1.3

Let \(G\) be a group and \(N\) be a subgroup of \(G\). Then the following statements are equivalent.

  1. The subgroup \(N\) is normal in \(G\).

  2. For all \(g\in G\), \(gNg^{-1}\subset N\).

  3. For all \(g\in G\), \(gNg^{-1}=N\).

Natural Homomorphism

Let \(H\) be a normal subgroup of \(G\). The natural or canonical homomorphism \(\phi:G\rightarrow G/H\) is defined by \(\phi(g)=gH\).

This is a homomorphism because

\[ \phi(g_1)\phi(g_2)=(g_1H)(g_2H)=g_1g_2H=\phi(g_1g_2). \]

  • Onto: Every coset \(gH\) is the image of \(g\).
  • Kernel: \(\phi(g)=H\) exactly when \(g\in H\), so \(\ker\phi=H\).

Thus every normal subgroup is the kernel of a homomorphism.

Two Images of a group

For a homomorphism \(\psi:G\rightarrow H\), let \(K=\ker\psi\) and let \(\phi:G\rightarrow G/K\) be the canonical homomorphism.

\[ \begin{array}{ccc} G & \xrightarrow{\ \psi\ } & \psi(G)\\ {\scriptstyle\phi}\downarrow & &\\ G/K & & \end{array} \]

What is the relationship between the two images \(\psi(G)\) and \(G/K\)?

Commutative Diagrams

A diagram commutes when following different directed paths with the same starting and ending objects gives the same map.

For a homomorphism \(\psi:G\rightarrow H\), let \(K=\ker\psi\) and let \(\phi:G\rightarrow G/K\) be the canonical homomorphism.

\[ \begin{array}{ccc} G & \xrightarrow{\ \psi\ } & \psi(G)\\ {\scriptstyle\phi}\downarrow & \nearrow{\scriptstyle\eta} &\\ G/K & & \end{array} \]

Commutativity means \(\psi=\eta\phi\).

Following the Paths

Start with any \(g\in G\). There are two directed paths from \(G\) to \(\psi(G)\):

  • Direct path: \(g\mapsto\psi(g)\).
  • Path through the quotient: \(g\mapsto gK\mapsto\eta(gK)\).

The First Isomorphism Theorem supplies the isomorphism \(\eta:G/K\rightarrow\psi(G)\) defined by \(\eta(gK)=\psi(g)\).

Therefore, both paths give the same result:

\[ \eta(\phi(g))=\eta(gK)=\psi(g). \]

The diagram records how \(\psi\) factors through the quotient group \(G/K\).

Theorem 11.2.1

First Isomorphism Theorem

If \(\psi:G\rightarrow H\) is a group homomorphism with \(K=\ker\psi\), then \(K\) is normal in \(G\).

Let \(\phi:G\rightarrow G/K\) be the canonical homomorphism.

Then there exists a unique isomorphism \(\eta:G/K\rightarrow\psi(G)\) such that \(\psi=\eta\phi\).

\[ G/\ker\psi\cong\psi(G). \]

First Isomorphism Theorem: Commutative Diagram

Let \(\psi:G\rightarrow H\) be a group homomorphism and let \(K=\ker\psi\).

The First Isomorphism Theorem gives:

\[ \begin{array}{ccc} G & \xrightarrow{\ \psi\ } & \psi(G)\\ {\scriptstyle\phi}\downarrow & \nearrow{\scriptstyle\eta} &\\ G/K & & \end{array} \]

  • Canonical homomorphism: \(\phi(g)=gK\).
  • Induced isomorphism: \(\eta(gK)=\psi(g)\).

The diagram commutes because \(\eta(\phi(g))=\psi(g)\).

Proof of Theorem 11.2.1

We already know that \(K\) is normal in \(G\). Define \(\eta:G/K\rightarrow\psi(G)\) by \(\eta(gK)=\psi(g)\).

  • Well-defined: If \(g_1K=g_2K\), then \(g_2=g_1k\) for some \(k\in K\). Hence \(\psi(g_2)=\psi(g_1)\psi(k)=\psi(g_1)\).

  • Homomorphism: \(\eta(g_1K\,g_2K)=\psi(g_1g_2)=\psi(g_1)\psi(g_2)=\eta(g_1K)\eta(g_2K)\).

  • Onto: Every element \(\psi(g)\) of \(\psi(G)\) equals \(\eta(gK)\).

  • One-to-one: If \(\eta(g_1K)=\eta(g_2K)\), then \(\psi(g_1)=\psi(g_2)\). Thus \(\psi(g_1^{-1}g_2)=e\), so \(g_1^{-1}g_2\in K\) and \(g_1K=g_2K\). \(\square\)

Example: Finite Cyclic Groups

Let \(G=\langle g\rangle\) with \(|g|=m\). Define \(\psi:\mathbb Z\rightarrow G\) by \(\psi(n)=g^n\).

This is an onto homomorphism because every element of \(G\) is a power of \(g\), and

\[ \psi(a)\psi(b)=g^ag^b=g^{a+b}=\psi(a+b). \]

Since \(g^n=e\) exactly when \(m\) divides \(n\), we have \(\ker\psi=m\mathbb Z\).

The First Isomorphism Theorem gives

\[ \mathbb Z/m\mathbb Z\cong G. \]

Every cyclic group of order \(m\) is isomorphic to \(\mathbb Z_m\).

Example: Infinite Cyclic Groups

Let \(G=\langle g\rangle\), where \(g\) has infinite order. Again define \(\psi:\mathbb Z\rightarrow G\) by \(\psi(n)=g^n\).

  • \(\psi\) is an onto homomorphism.
  • Since \(g\) has infinite order, \(g^n=e\) only when \(n=0\).
  • Therefore, \(\ker\psi=\{0\}\).

The First Isomorphism Theorem gives

\[ \mathbb Z/\{0\}\cong G, \qquad\text{so}\qquad \mathbb Z\cong G. \]

Up to isomorphism, the only cyclic groups are \(\mathbb Z\) and \(\mathbb Z_m\).

Example: Identifying \(S_3/A_3\)

The sign homomorphism \(\psi:S_3\rightarrow\mathbb Z_2\) sends even permutations to \(0\) and odd permutations to \(1\). We showed this was a homomorphism previously.

  • Onto: The identity maps to \(0\), and a transposition maps to \(1\).
  • Kernel: \(\ker\psi=A_3=\{e,(123),(132)\}\).

The First Isomorphism Theorem gives

\[ S_3/A_3\cong\mathbb Z_2. \]

Implications: A Strategy for Identifying Groups

To identify a quotient group \(G/N\):

  1. Find a homomorphism \(\psi:G\rightarrow H\) into a familiar group.
  2. Show that \(\ker\psi=N\).
  3. Identify the image \(\psi(G)\).

Then the First Isomorphism Theorem gives \(G/N\cong\psi(G)\).

  • Finding \(N\) as a kernel also proves that \(N\) is normal.
  • If \(\psi\) is onto, then \(G/N\cong H\).
  • If \(\ker\psi=\{e\}\), then \(G\cong\psi(G)\).

Theorem 11.2.3

Second Isomorphism Theorem

Let \(H\) be a subgroup of a group \(G\), not necessarily normal in \(G\), and \(N\) a normal subgroup of \(G\).

Then \(HN\) is a subgroup of \(G\), \(H\cap N\) is a normal subgroup of \(H\), and

\[ H/H\cap N\cong HN/N. \]

Second Isomorphism Theorem: Commutative Diagram

Let \(H\leq G\) and \(N\) be normal in \(G\). Define \(\psi:H\rightarrow HN/N\) by \(\psi(h)=hN\).

Since \(\ker\psi=H\cap N\), the First Isomorphism Theorem gives:

\[ \begin{array}{ccc} H & \xrightarrow{\ \psi\ } & HN/N\\ {\scriptstyle\phi}\downarrow & \nearrow{\scriptstyle\eta} &\\ H/(H\cap N) & & \end{array} \]

  • Canonical homomorphism: \(\phi(h)=h(H\cap N)\).
  • Induced isomorphism: \(\eta(h(H\cap N))=hN\).

The diagram commutes because \(\eta(\phi(h))=hN=\psi(h)\).

Proof of Theorem 11.2.3

First, \(HN=\{hn:h\in H,n\in N\}\) contains \(e\). Since \(N\) is normal,

\[ \begin{aligned} (h_1n_1)(h_2n_2) &=h_1h_2(h_2^{-1}n_1h_2)n_2\in HN,\\ (hn)^{-1} &=n^{-1}h^{-1}=h^{-1}(hn^{-1}h^{-1})\in HN. \end{aligned} \]

Thus \(HN\) is a subgroup of \(G\).

For \(h\in H\) and \(n\in H\cap N\), the element \(h^{-1}nh\) belongs to both \(H\) and \(N\). Hence \(H\cap N\) is normal in \(H\).

Define \(\phi:H\rightarrow HN/N\) by \(\phi(h)=hN\). It is onto because \(hnN=hN\), and it is a homomorphism because \(\phi(hh')=hh'N=hNh'N=\phi(h)\phi(h')\).

Since \(\ker\phi=\{h\in H:h\in N\}=H\cap N\), the First Isomorphism Theorem gives \(H/H\cap N\cong HN/N\). \(\square\)

Theorem 11.2.4

Correspondence Theorem

Let \(N\) be a normal subgroup of a group \(G\).

Then \(H\mapsto H/N\) is a one-to-one correspondence between the set of subgroups \(H\) of \(G\) containing \(N\) and the set of subgroups of \(G/N\).

Furthermore, the normal subgroups of \(G\) containing \(N\) correspond to normal subgroups of \(G/N\).

Proof of Theorem 11.2.4

Let \(\pi:G\rightarrow G/N\) be the canonical homomorphism.

  • Subgroups correspond: If \(N\subseteq H\leq G\), then \(H/N=\pi(H)\) is a subgroup of \(G/N\). If \(S\leq G/N\), then \(H=\pi^{-1}(S)\) is a subgroup containing \(N\), and \(H/N=S\).

  • The correspondence is one-to-one: For every subgroup \(H\) containing \(N\), \(\pi^{-1}(H/N)=H\). Thus taking images and taking inverse images give inverse correspondences.

  • Normality passes to the quotient: If \(H\) is normal in \(G\), the map \(G/N\rightarrow G/H\) defined by \(gN\mapsto gH\) is a homomorphism with kernel \(H/N\). Hence \(H/N\) is normal in \(G/N\).

  • Normality lifts from the quotient: If \(H/N\) is normal in \(G/N\), then \(H=\pi^{-1}(H/N)\) is normal in \(G\) by Proposition 11.1.4(4). \(\square\)

Theorem 11.2.5

Third Isomorphism Theorem

Let \(G\) be a group and \(N\) and \(H\) be normal subgroups of \(G\) with \(N\subset H\).

Then

\[ G/H\cong\frac{G/N}{H/N}. \]

Third Isomorphism Theorem: Commutative Diagram

Let \(N\) and \(H\) be normal subgroups of \(G\) with \(N\subseteq H\). Define \(\rho:G/N\rightarrow G/H\) by \(\rho(gN)=gH\).

Since \(\ker\rho=H/N\), the First Isomorphism Theorem gives:

\[ \begin{array}{ccc} G/N & \xrightarrow{\ \rho\ } & G/H\\ {\scriptstyle\phi}\downarrow & \nearrow{\scriptstyle\eta} &\\ (G/N)/(H/N) & & \end{array} \]

  • Canonical homomorphism: \(\phi(gN)=(gN)(H/N)\).
  • Induced isomorphism: \(\eta((gN)(H/N))=gH\).

The diagram commutes because \(\eta(\phi(gN))=gH=\rho(gN)\).

Proof of Theorem 11.2.5

Define \(\rho:G/N\rightarrow G/H\) by \(\rho(gN)=gH\).

  • Well-defined: If \(g_1N=g_2N\), then \(g_1^{-1}g_2\in N\subseteq H\), so \(g_1H=g_2H\).

  • Homomorphism: \(\rho(g_1N\,g_2N)=g_1g_2H=(g_1H)(g_2H)\).

  • Onto: Every coset \(gH\) is the image of \(gN\).

  • Kernel: \(\rho(gN)=H\) exactly when \(g\in H\). Thus \(\ker\rho=H/N\).

By the First Isomorphism Theorem,

\[ \frac{G/N}{H/N}\cong G/H. \]

Therefore \(G/H\cong(G/N)/(H/N)\). \(\square\)
